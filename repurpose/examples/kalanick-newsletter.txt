What Kalanick actually learned at Uber

David Senra finally got Travis Kalanick on the record for two hours, and the result is less a victory lap than an operating manual. The frame he opens with: "The only constraint on our imagination is management capacity." He treats growth as an inequality he calls the meta problem. Your rate of problem-solving must stay at or above your rate of problem creation; the moment it doesn't, you stop entering new markets until the backlog clears. Going to China was, in his words, creating a problem whose nature you cannot fully predict, and the problems "start coming ashore" six months later.

The competitive sections are the sharpest. His account of the Didi war comes down to one phrase, "efficiency outstrips subsidy": as networks scale, the subsidy a rival can afford shrinks relative to the efficiency edge of the better operator. He credits that mechanic, down to sign-up friction and driver routing, for why Lyft stayed smaller than Uber.

And his fundraising doctrine deserves its own card: "I never get attached to a price. I get attached to a process." Pressed by Bill Gurley in 2014 to take the first term sheet at a $6 billion valuation, he ran his multi-room auction instead and closed at $17.5 billion pre two months later. At peak Uber the process ran five rooms simultaneously for a week, sorted by check size, producing a literal demand curve for the round.

If you only have ten minutes, start at the taxi-medallion history, his case that Uber's real opponent was "a government-condoned cartel that outlaws competition."
