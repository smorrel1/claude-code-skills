# Persuasion in short emails: the fuller reference

The checklist in SKILL.md ("Persuasion check") is the working tool. This file
explains where each line comes from, and which famous techniques were left out
because they do not survive in a 50 to 125 word written email.

**Ground rules, unchanged:** honesty first; never invent scarcity, deadlines,
demand, social proof or engagement; nothing that reads as a trick; never make
the email longer. Run after the thread lookup and org-sense, before humanizer.

Chapter references are given only where they are reliable. Where an idea runs
through a whole book, the book alone is cited.

## 1. Dale Carnegie, How to Win Friends and Influence People (revised edition, 1981)

| # | Idea | In an email | Where |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Arouse in the other person an eager want | Open with what they gain | Part 1 Ch 3 |
| 2 | Give honest, sincere appreciation | One specific, true thank-you, never flattery | Part 1 Ch 2 |
| 3 | Remember and use their name | Their name, their project, their deadline | Part 2 Ch 3 |
| 4 | Talk in terms of their interests | Frame the ask in their world, not ours | Part 2 Ch 5 |
| 5 | Let them feel the idea is theirs | Ask rather than tell | Part 3 Ch 7 |
| 6 | Let the other person save face | Offer an easy no | Part 4 Ch 5 |

## 2. Robert Cialdini, Influence (new and expanded edition, 2021) and Pre-Suasion (2016)

| # | Principle | In an email | Where |
|---|-----------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Reciprocation | Give something useful first, where there is something real to give | Influence Ch 2 |
| 2 | Liking | Shared ground, genuine compliments | Influence Ch 3 |
| 3 | Social proof | Who like them has done it, only if true | Influence Ch 4 |
| 4 | Authority | A credible, checkable source | Influence Ch 5 |
| 5 | Scarcity | Only a real limit. Invented scarcity is a lie | Influence Ch 6 |
| 6 | Commitment and consistency | Ask for a small first step | Influence Ch 7 |
| 7 | Unity | A real shared identity: KCL, the same grant, the same patients | Influence Ch 8 |
| 8 | Pre-suasion | The opening line primes how the ask is read | Pre-Suasion |

## 3. Chris Voss, Never Split the Difference (2016)

Built for live negotiation. These carry over to writing:

| # | Tool | In an email | Where |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Tactical empathy | Show you understand their position before asking | Ch 3 |
| 2 | Labelling | "It sounds like the timing is tight." Names a feeling without arguing with it | Ch 3 |
| 3 | Accusation audit | Name the objection first: "This may look like one more AI pitch." It takes the sting out | Ch 3 |
| 4 | No-oriented questions | "Would it be a bad idea to...?" A no feels safe, so people engage | Ch 4 |
| 5 | Getting to "that's right" | Summarise their view so well they agree with it. Works in a reply, rarely in a cold email | Ch 5 |
| 6 | Calibrated questions | "How would you want to...?", "What would make this worth it?" They do the thinking and own the answer | Ch 7 |

**Mirroring** (Ch 2, repeating their last few words) works on a call. In writing,
the equivalent is quoting their own phrase back once. More than once reads as copying.

## 4. Daniel Pink, To Sell Is Human (2012)

| # | Idea | In an email | Where |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Attunement | See it from their side before writing | Ch 4 |
| 2 | Clarity | A clear, specific next step beats a vague one | Ch 6 |

## 5. Neil Rackham, SPIN Selling (1988)

Rackham's research on large, complex sales found that questions about the
**implications** of a problem and the **payoff** of solving it predict success,
while listing features does not. In an email: one line on what the problem costs
them, then what solving it gives them. Never a list of product features.

## 6. Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson, The Challenger Sale (2011)

Their research says the strongest sellers **teach** the customer something new
about their own problem, tailor it, and are comfortable steering. In an email:
one line of genuine insight they may not have ("Most localisation errors in DBT
happen on dense breasts, not on the hardest cases"). Only if it is true and
supportable.

## 7. Jonah Berger, The Catalyst (2020)

The best fit for email. Berger's case is that change comes from **removing
barriers**, not pushing harder. His five barriers (REDUCE):

| # | Barrier | In an email |
|---|---------|-------------|
| 1 | Reactance | Pushing makes people push back. Ask, offer choices, let them decide |
| 2 | Endowment | People hold on to what they have. Show the cost of doing nothing, gently |
| 3 | Distance | Too big an ask is rejected. Ask for the smallest real step |
| 4 | Uncertainty | Lower the risk: a pilot, a 15-minute call, an easy exit |
| 5 | Corroborating evidence | One source rarely convinces. More than one independent, true source does |

## 8. Tony Robbins (Awaken the Giant Within, 1991, and his "human needs" model)

| # | Idea | In an email |
|---|------|-------------|
| 1 | State drives behaviour | Write when calm and rested. A tired or rushed email reads needy. Ties to org-sense rule 9, timing |
| 2 | The need for certainty | Make the next step clear and low risk |
| 3 | The need for significance | Say why them in particular |
| 4 | The need for connection | Warmth, a real shared tie |

## 9. Oren Klaff, Pitch Anything (2011)

Mostly a live-pitch method. The part that carries to email is **frame control**:
neediness kills a pitch. In an email, do not over-explain, do not chase twice in
a week, do not apologise for asking. State the offer plainly and let them come to it.

## 10. Zig Ziglar (Secrets of Closing the Sale, 1984)

Ziglar's core idea is that selling succeeds when it sincerely helps the other
person get what they want. In an email that is check 1, their gain first, done
honestly.

## Left out, and why

| # | Technique | Why it is not in the checklist |
|---|-----------|-------------------------------|
| 1 | Artificial urgency or scarcity | Dishonest, and found out it costs the relationship |
| 2 | Long story-led openings | Too long for a 50 to 125 word email |
| 3 | Klaff's status games, "prizing" | Read as arrogant in writing |
| 4 | Repeated mirroring | Reads as copying on the page |
| 5 | Hard closes (Ziglar's closing scripts) | Pressure in writing triggers reactance (Berger) |
